# Aluminum Windows and Doors Prices: Why Similar Quotes Can Be Miles Apart

## The price gap is usually a spec gap

After comparing aluminum window and door quotes for renovations, new builds, and commercial jobs, one pattern keeps repeating: the biggest difference between a cheap quote and an expensive one is rarely the opening size. It is the specification hiding behind the number. A budget slider, a thermally broken casement, and a bi-fold door can all be sold as the same product category, but they are not the same product in any meaningful cost sense.

For a quick benchmark, the [price range guide](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-windows-and-doors-prices/) gives the broad bands, but broad bands only help once the frame type, glass package, finish, and install scope are fixed.

A 1200 x 1000 mm aluminum window might come in under $700 supply-only in AUD when it uses a standard frame, single glazing, and basic hardware. Put the same opening into a thermally broken frame with low-E double glazing, upgraded locks, and a custom powder coat, and the number can easily double. The opening did not change. The engineering inside it did.

### Why size matters less than most buyers expect

Size absolutely affects cost, but it is only one layer of the equation. Bigger windows need more aluminum extrusion and more glass, and bigger doors need heavier tracks and stronger hardware. That part is obvious. What is less obvious is that two openings with the same dimensions can still land in different price tiers because one is fixed and the other opens, one uses standard glass and the other needs safety glass, or one fits a stock finish while the other requires a custom color run.

A fixed window is the cheapest version of a given opening because it has no moving parts. Add a sash, hinges, rollers, stays, or a multi-point lock, and the frame stops being a simple enclosure and becomes a mechanical system. That is where the quote starts moving.

For doors, the spread is even larger. A sliding door is already more expensive than a comparable window because it carries more weight and has to manage traffic, weather, and security. A bi-fold door pushes that further because every panel adds hinges, rollers, seals, and fabrication time. Same width. Completely different build.

### The four specification choices that move the number fastest

If a quote jumps, these are usually the first places to look.

- **Thermal break or no thermal break**  
  A thermally broken frame has a non-conductive barrier between the inner and outer aluminum sections. That improves energy performance and condensation control, but it adds cost. In many projects, the premium is roughly 15% to 25% over a non-broken frame.

- **Single glazing, double glazing, or a performance glass package**  
  Glass is often the biggest swing factor. Standard single glazing is cheapest. Double glazing raises the glass cost substantially. Add low-E coatings, argon fill, laminated safety layers, or acoustic performance, and the number rises again.

- **Standard finish or custom finish**  
  Popular powder coat colors are usually priced efficiently because they are produced in volume. A custom color match, woodgrain look, or marine-grade coating can add a meaningful premium. Coastal projects often need the more expensive finish whether the buyer wants it or not.

- **Basic hardware or premium hardware**  
  Budget hardware often feels fine on day one and disappointing a few years later. Better rollers, locks, hinges, and friction stays cost more up front but typically buy smoother operation and longer service life. On casement and awning windows, hardware quality can swing the quote more than the frame itself.

### Supply-only and installed are not competing numbers

This is where many homeowners get misled. One quote may be supply-only and another fully installed. Those numbers are not comparable, even if the window looks identical on paper.

Supply-only usually covers the fabricated frame, glass, seals, locks, and screens if specified. Installed pricing adds removal of old products, labor, fixing, waterproofing, detailing around the opening, disposal, access equipment, and sometimes structural work. On a simple like-for-like replacement, installation can add a few hundred dollars per opening. On a large door, new opening, or difficult site access, it can add thousands.

That difference matters because a cheap supply-only quote can easily become the most expensive option once installation, flashing, trim, and site corrections are added back in. The opposite happens too: a higher supply-only quote can be better value if it already includes the labor, certification, and finishing work that the lower quote has excluded.

The cleanest comparison is simple: compare only supply-only to supply-only, or only installed to installed. Mixing the two creates fake savings.

### What to ask for before comparing any quote

A proper comparison needs the same variables on both sides. If one supplier leaves something vague, the quote is incomplete.

Ask for:

- exact opening dimensions
- frame depth and profile type
- fixed, sliding, awning, casement, or bi-fold configuration
- single glazing, double glazing, or upgraded glass build-up
- low-E coating, argon fill, lamination, or toughening
- standard finish or custom powder coat
- hardware brand and lock type
- supply-only or fully installed scope
- removal of existing units
- finishing, sealant, and making-good inclusions
- compliance requirements, if relevant
- screens, thresholds, and accessories

If two quotes do not match on those items, the lower number is not automatically the better deal. It may simply be the leaner specification.

> The cheapest quote is only cheaper when it includes the same frame, the same glass, the same hardware, and the same installation scope.

### Where paying more is the right move

Some projects can absorb a lower-spec product without much regret. Others cannot.

Coastal homes are a good example. Salt air punishes coatings, fasteners, and exposed hardware. A slightly more expensive marine-grade finish can prevent corrosion that would otherwise show up years earlier than expected.

Energy-conscious homes are another case. If the build is trying to reduce cooling loads in a hot climate or heating losses in a cooler one, a thermally broken frame with better glazing can do real work. The upfront premium may be visible on the quote, but so is the reduction in long-term energy waste and condensation issues.

Large doors also justify stronger specs. The bigger the moving panel, the more the quote depends on roller quality, track design, and structural support. A cheap door that drags, rattles, or leaks is not a bargain. It is a repair schedule.

The same logic applies to commercial projects. Higher traffic means more cycles, more wear, and more demand for repeatable performance. In that setting, the lowest number often reflects the shortest service life.

### The easiest way to read a quote correctly

Think of each quote as a list of decisions, not just a price.

A good quote tells you:

- what the product is
- what the product is made of
- how it performs
- how it will be installed
- what is excluded

If any of those are missing, the number is incomplete.

That is why broad ranges should be treated as starting points. The real cost is determined when the specification becomes real. Once the glass build-up, frame type, finish, and installation scope are fixed, the quote stops being a mystery and starts being a decision.

The most useful question is not, 'Why is one supplier cheaper?' It is, 'What did the cheaper quote leave out, downgrade, or simplify?' Answer that honestly, and the pricing gap usually explains itself.
