# Whole-Window U-Value in Aluminium Windows: What Most Specs Miss

## The Number That Actually Tells the Truth

The most expensive mistake in aluminium window specification is treating the glass as the product. The building never experiences the glass by itself. It experiences the whole assembly: the aluminium frame, the thermal break, the spacer at the perimeter, the seal, and the glass unit working together. That is why [glazing aluminium windows](https://meichenwindows.com.au/glazing-aluminium-windows/) cannot be reduced to a single glass-only number without losing the part that most affects real performance.

A spec that quotes only Ug can still look impressive on paper while delivering mediocre comfort on site. That happens because Ug measures the centre of the glass only. It excludes the frame and the edge-of-glass zone, which are exactly where aluminium systems lose a surprising amount of heat. In a real installation, those losses are not marginal. They can decide whether a room feels stable in winter or sits in a drafty, cold pocket around the opening.

## Why Glass-Only Numbers Are So Seductive

Ug is easy to market because it is the best number a glazing supplier can put in front of a buyer. It comes from the part of the system with the most room for improvement, so it often looks excellent. A low-E double-glazed unit with argon might show a Ug around 1.1 W/m²K. On a datasheet, that sounds close to high-performance envelope territory.

But a window is not a sheet of glass. Once that IGU is installed, the frame begins to matter just as much as the cavity inside the glass. The thermal break width, the frame geometry, the spacer material, and the glass-to-frame ratio all influence the actual installed result. A narrow opening with a heavy frame can perform much worse than a large opening with more glass area, even if both use the same IGU.

That is why comparing glass-only numbers across projects is misleading. Two windows can share the same glazed unit and still deliver very different outcomes because their frames are different. The framing is not decoration around the glass. It is part of the thermal path.

## Aluminium Makes the Frame Problem Harder to Ignore

Aluminium is structurally excellent and thermally unforgiving. Its conductivity is high enough that an unbroken profile acts like a bridge between the interior and exterior. In practice, that means the frame can pull heat out of the room almost as efficiently as it supports the opening.

The difference between a thermally broken and a non-thermally broken profile is not subtle. A strong IGU installed in a non-broken aluminium frame can underdeliver badly because the frame becomes the dominant loss path. Put the same IGU into a thermally broken system and the whole-window result can improve dramatically without changing the glass at all.

A simple way to think about it is this: the glass might be excellent at resisting heat flow through its centre, but the window still leaks through its perimeter. The more frame you have relative to glass, the more severe that penalty becomes. Small windows, punched openings, and operable sashes all magnify the effect.

The governing formula makes the point plainly:

`Uw = (Ag×Ug + Af×Uf + Lg×Ψ) / Aw`

That is the heart of the issue. Uw is a weighted result, not an average of good intentions. Ug, Uf, and the edge term Ψ all contribute. If the frame term is poor, the final number rises fast. A specification that ignores the frame is, by definition, leaving part of the calculation out.

## The Edge Zone Is Where Comfort Breaks Down

Most occupants do not complain that a window has a bad Ug. They complain that it feels cold, that condensation collects at the edges, or that they can feel a chill when they sit nearby. Those symptoms usually point to the perimeter of the unit, not the centre of the glass.

The spacer bar matters here. An aluminium spacer conducts heat readily and can create a cold ring around the edge of the IGU. A warm-edge spacer reduces that effect by cutting the conductive path between panes. In practical terms, that means a warmer inner edge, less condensation risk, and a window that feels less harsh on a cold morning.

That is why the most effective systems are matched systems: thermally broken frame, low-conductivity spacer, and a glazed unit chosen for the climate and orientation. If one part is left behind, the whole assembly pays for it. A premium IGU can be dragged down by a cheap spacer. A good spacer can be undermined by a poor frame. The system only works when the edge conditions are treated as part of the design, not as an afterthought.

## What a Specification Should Demand Instead

A serious specification for [complete window performance](https://meichenwindows.com.au/glazing-aluminium-windows/) starts with the installed assembly, not the glass datasheet. The questions that matter are practical ones:

- What is the whole-window U-value, not just the centre-of-glass value?
- What frame system is being used, and is it thermally broken?
- What spacer type is in the IGU?
- What size window was the performance tested at?
- Are the quoted numbers for the exact configuration being supplied?

That last point is where many submittals go soft. A manufacturer can quote a favorable number for a generic or idealized sample, but the actual window size, opening type, and frame depth can shift the final result. Performance on paper is only useful if it corresponds to the unit that will be installed.

For aluminium systems, this matters even more because the frame has such a strong influence on the final outcome. A well-designed frame can make a mid-range IGU perform well. A poor frame can make a premium IGU look ordinary.

## When Better Glass Is Enough, and When It Is Not

There are cases where upgrading the glass genuinely pays off. If a project already has a strong thermally broken frame and a sensible spacer, moving from standard double glazing to a better Low-E build-up can improve comfort and energy use in a meaningful way. The window is already behaving as a system, so the glass upgrade is a real gain.

The opposite is also true. If the frame is non-thermally broken or the edge detail is weak, chasing a better IGU often produces diminishing returns. The brochure number improves, but the room barely notices. At that point, money spent on a stronger frame or a better spacer usually buys more comfort than a more expensive glass package.

That is the core lesson most specifications miss. Glass performance matters, but only inside the assembly that holds it. A window is not a glass order with hardware attached. It is a thermal device whose weakest link sets the result.

## The Right Question

The right question is not, What glass is in it?

The right question is, What does the whole window do?

Until a specification answers that, the numbers are only telling half the story.
