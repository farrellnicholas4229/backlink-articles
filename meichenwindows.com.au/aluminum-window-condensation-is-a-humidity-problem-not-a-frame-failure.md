# Aluminum Window Condensation Is a Humidity Problem, Not a Frame Failure

## The frame is visible, so it gets blamed

Water on an aluminum frame feels like proof that the window has failed. The droplets are sitting on the frame, after all, so the conclusion seems obvious. In practice, [aluminum window condensation](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-windows-condensation/) is usually the place where indoor humidity and a cold surface finally meet. The frame did not create the water; it made the room's moisture visible.

> A wet frame is usually a temperature problem wearing a window's name tag.

That distinction matters because replacement solves one problem and ventilation solves another. If the diagnosis is wrong, money gets spent in the wrong place.

## Condensation begins at dew point

Condensation forms when air cools to the point where it can no longer hold the moisture it contains. At that temperature, known as the dew point, water vapor turns into liquid on the first cold surface it touches.

A simple example makes the math clear. Indoor air at 21°C and 60% relative humidity has a dew point of roughly 12.6°C. If the interior face of a window frame drops to 11°C or 12°C overnight, droplets will form even though nothing is leaking. The same thing happens on a cold soda can or a bathroom mirror.

Aluminum gets blamed because it is an excellent conductor of heat. When outdoor temperatures fall, an aluminum frame loses warmth quickly and the inside face can become colder than the surrounding room air. The window is not making water. It is allowing the room's moisture to condense where the surface temperature falls below dew point.

## Why aluminum exposes the issue faster than other materials

Comparisons usually get framed as aluminum versus uPVC versus timber, but that comparison misses the deeper point. The materials do not behave the same way because they do not conduct heat the same way.

- Aluminum moves heat rapidly, so the interior face can cool fast on a winter night.
- uPVC slows heat flow because its profile is far less conductive.
- Timber naturally resists heat transfer better than bare aluminum.

That difference explains why an unbroken aluminum frame often shows condensation sooner than other frame types. The visible moisture is not evidence that aluminum is broken; it is evidence that the frame is cold enough to reveal what the room is doing.

A thermally broken aluminum frame changes the picture by interrupting the metal path between the inside and outside sections. A low-conductivity strip, usually polyamide, keeps the interior surface warmer. That improvement lowers risk, but it does not repeal dew point. A room that sits at 70% relative humidity will still challenge almost any frame.

## The room can be the real culprit

Across home inspections, the same pattern appears over and over: one window in a house stays dry while another fills with moisture. The frame did not change. The room did.

Bedrooms are a good example. Two adults sleeping in a closed room can add a surprising amount of moisture overnight through breathing alone. If the door stays shut, the heating is turned down, and the vents are closed, humidity climbs while the window surface cools. Condensation shows up at dawn, usually on the lower edge of the frame where cold settles first.

Bathrooms create a different version of the same problem. A shower can spike humidity in minutes. Kitchens do it more gradually through cooking steam. New homes add another variable: building materials like plaster, concrete, and timber release moisture as they dry out, which can keep indoor humidity elevated for months.

The frame is reacting to those conditions. It is not generating them.

## Three very different kinds of moisture

Not all window condensation means the same thing, and confusing the types leads to bad decisions.

- **Inside condensation**: moisture on the room side of the glass or frame. This usually means indoor humidity is too high for the surface temperature.
- **Outside condensation**: moisture on the exterior pane. This often means the glazing is insulating well and the outer surface is cooling below outdoor dew point.
- **Between the panes**: fog or droplets trapped inside a double-glazed unit. That points to seal failure, which is a real product problem and needs attention.

Only the last one is a structural failure of the sealed unit. The first two are environmental and thermal behavior showing up on the glass.

## What a correct diagnosis looks like

A better question than "Is the window bad?" is "What condition is the window revealing?"

A practical check usually starts with three things:

1. Measure indoor relative humidity, especially in bedrooms and bathrooms.
2. Note where the moisture appears: inside, outside, or between panes.
3. Check whether the frame is thermally broken or a continuous metal section.

If the moisture wipes away and returns only during cold, humid mornings, the issue is almost always room conditions plus a cold surface. If it remains trapped between the panes, the glazing unit has failed. If the outside pane fogs up before sunrise, the window may actually be performing very well.

This is why blanket advice like "replace the windows" often misses the point. A sound aluminum frame can still collect water if the indoor air is too humid. A poor seal can fail even if the frame itself is fine. The right fix depends on which part of the system is actually under stress.

## Why the distinction saves money and trouble

Mislabeling condensation as frame failure leads to unnecessary replacements, especially in homes where the real issue is high indoor humidity or inadequate ventilation. On the other hand, dismissing trapped moisture between panes as ordinary condensation leaves a failed sealed unit in place and keeps energy performance slipping further.

The useful mindset is simple: the frame is a surface, not a source. When it sweats, it is telling you something about the room, the weather, or the thermal design of the window system. Read the signal correctly, and the fix becomes obvious.

A dry frame in winter is not magic. It is the result of a warm enough interior surface, controlled humidity, and a frame design that does not create a hard thermal bridge. When those pieces line up, aluminum stops looking like the problem and starts doing what it was meant to do: hold a slim, durable opening without becoming the coldest object in the room.
