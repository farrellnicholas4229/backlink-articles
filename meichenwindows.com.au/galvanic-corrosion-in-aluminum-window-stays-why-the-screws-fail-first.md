# Galvanic Corrosion in Aluminum Window Stays: Why the Screws Fail First

## The Corrosion Problem Starts at the Fastener

A corroded aluminum window stay is often blamed as a worn-out part, but the visible damage is usually the result of a much smaller event: a stainless screw, a damp frame, and a joint that held moisture long enough to become a battery. When the white powder shows up around the fixing points, the stay arm is usually just the messenger. The fastener interface is where the aluminum starts to lose metal atom by atom.

### Why the hardware looks white instead of red

Rust belongs to iron. Aluminum fails differently. Once the oxide layer is breached and moisture is present, the metal produces a pale, chalky corrosion product that can look like dried paint or salt. That residue is a clue. If the damage is centered on screw heads, countersinks, and the edge of the mounting plate, the problem is almost never simple weathering. It is galvanic corrosion: two dissimilar metals connected through moisture, with aluminum acting as the sacrificial metal.

### The metal pairing that causes the trouble

In dry air, nothing much happens. Add condensation, irrigation spray, or sea salt and the circuit closes. Stainless steel is more noble than aluminum, so the aluminum gives up electrons and pits around the contact patch. Even grade 316 stainless, which performs well in marine environments, still needs isolation when it touches bare aluminum. Better corrosion resistance does not equal compatibility.

That is why a stay can look sound from across the room while the fixing points are quietly failing underneath. The screw may still be bright. The arm may still open and close. The frame material around the hole is what disappears first.

### Why coastal homes see it sooner

The same hardware can last years inland and fail in a fraction of that time near the ocean. The difference is not just salt content in the air. Salt lowers the electrical resistance of the moisture film sitting on the joint, which makes the galvanic reaction easier to sustain. Condensation also matters. Windows that cool at night and warm in the morning keep pulling moisture into the screw line, especially on east- and south-facing elevations.

In a sheltered inland house, the first signs may take several years to show. In a coastal property with regular wind-driven salt exposure, the first chalky bloom around the fixings can appear in one or two wet seasons. Once the corrosion starts, repeated wet-dry cycling makes it worse because each cycle refreshes the electrolyte and opens micro-gaps under the coating.

### The failure pattern that gives it away

Galvanic corrosion has a look and a location. It rarely spreads evenly across the whole stay. Instead, it concentrates where the metals meet and where water lingers.

- white bloom around screw holes
- pitted aluminum around countersinks
- fasteners that loosen after repeated wet-dry cycles
- powder coat lifting at edges and cut lines
- a stay arm that still looks acceptable but no longer holds firmly because the fixing points have softened

Once the hole has enlarged, tightening only accelerates the problem. Over-torquing wipes out the remaining thread wall. Touch-up paint and spray lubricants do not solve the chemistry either; they may hide the symptoms while moisture keeps working under the surface.

### Why coatings help only if they stay intact

Powder coat and anodizing are valuable barriers, but they are not magic armor. The moment a screw cuts through the coating or a drill bit exposes raw metal, the protection becomes local instead of complete. Around that tiny breach, capillary action holds water longer than people expect.

That is why the damage often starts at the bottom of a countersink or along the edge of a bracket rather than across the broad face of the frame. The exposed spot is small, but the galvanic current does not need a large opening. It only needs continuous contact, moisture, and a path between the metals.

### Stopping the reaction means breaking the circuit

A proper fix does not begin with a stronger screw. It begins with removing the metal contact that is creating the battery effect.

- Match metals whenever possible: aluminum stay on aluminum frame
- Isolate stainless fasteners with nylon washers or nonconductive bushings
- Use barrier paste or neutral-cure sealant at the contact point
- Keep water out of the fixing line by preserving drainage and avoiding trapped debris
- Rinse salt off regularly in coastal environments

When replacement is unavoidable, choose [matched aluminum window stays](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-window-stays/) and treat the fasteners as part of the system, not an afterthought. A well-made stay with incompatible hardware can fail faster than a mid-grade stay installed with proper isolation.

### Repair versus replacement

Light surface corrosion can often be cleaned back to a stable condition if the screw holes are still sound. Remove the hardware, brush away loose oxide, dry the joint thoroughly, and refit with isolated fasteners. If the hole has gone oval, the screw spins, or the aluminum crumbles when the fastener comes out, the structure around the fixing has already been compromised.

At that point, an oversized screw only buys time. The stay depends on solid metal behind it. If the frame wall has been eaten away, the load path is gone, and the next gust of wind or hard opening cycle will find the weak spot again.

### The rule that prevents most failures

> Any time dissimilar metal touches aluminum and moisture can reach the joint, assume galvanic corrosion unless the circuit has been deliberately broken.

That rule explains most premature failures on window hardware. It also explains why a stay in a protected interior room may look perfect after a decade while the same product on a salty, windward elevation starts to pit around the fixings far sooner.

The visible stay is not usually the weak link. The hidden joint is.
