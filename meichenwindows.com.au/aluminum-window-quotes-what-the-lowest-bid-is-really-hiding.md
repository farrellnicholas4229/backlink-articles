# Aluminum Window Quotes: What the Lowest Bid Is Really Hiding

## A quote is a scope document, not a price

In the quotes I've reviewed for homeowners, the lowest number almost always comes from the narrowest scope. That's the hidden trick behind many aluminum window bids. A $9,400 quote can look better than a $12,600 quote until the first change order appears. Once removal, disposal, flashing, trims, repainting, scaffolding, or GST are added back in, the cheap bid can become the expensive one.

A broader [average cost guide](https://meichenwindows.com.au/average-cost-of-aluminium-windows/) gives you a starting point, but the benchmark only matters after each quote is reduced to the same scope.

## Where low quotes save money

A low quote is not always dishonest. It is often just incomplete. Some suppliers quote the frame and glass only, assuming the rest is someone else's problem. Others include labor but leave access equipment, patching, or finishing as provisional items. That difference is enough to create a misleading gap of 15 to 30 percent on paper.

### The omissions that change the total

- Removal of old windows: often $150 to $400 per opening when the existing unit is hard to extract or the reveal is damaged.
- Disposal and waste handling: more noticeable on larger replacements, especially when old timber, glass, and packaging have to be carted away.
- Access equipment: scaffolding or a lift can add $800 to $3,500 on a small to medium project and more on a second-story home with poor side access.
- Making good: patching plaster, repairing render, resealing, and trimming out openings can add $100 to $300 per window.
- Finish work: painting internal reveals or matching external trim often lands after the windows are already in place.
- GST: a quote that leaves tax out looks cheaper until the final invoice arrives.

The real issue is that these are not optional luxuries. On many homes, they are the difference between a window that fits and a window that looks finished.

## Why the same window can produce different quotes

Two quotes can both be honest and still be impossible to compare if the assumptions differ. A supplier quoting a ground-floor, standard-size sliding window is pricing a simple fabrication and a quick install. A second supplier quoting the same opening may have measured a slightly out-of-square frame, checked the wall condition, allowed for sealant upgrades, and included a second installer for safe handling. The result is a higher number, but not necessarily a worse quote.

That is why the lowest aluminum window quote is rarely the best signal. A quote should be judged against the exact job, not against the other guy's headline number.

### A normal comparison looks like this

Imagine a 10-opening townhouse project in AUD:

- Quote A: $11,800
- Quote B: $15,200

At first glance, Quote A seems like the obvious win. But suppose Quote A excludes second-story access, external sealant, internal patching, and waste removal. Those extras add another $2,900 once the installer is on site.

Now the real total is $14,700.

Quote B, meanwhile, includes scaffold allowance, finishing, and heavier-duty frames for the larger rear openings. Suddenly the gap is only $500, and the cheaper-looking quote is the one with the biggest risk of variation claims.

That is the pattern that matters. Price differences are often really scope differences.

## The hidden cost categories that deserve line-by-line treatment

A serious quote should separate the job into parts that can be compared:

1. Supply of the window units
2. Installation labor
3. Access and safety equipment
4. Removal and disposal
5. Finishing and making good
6. Tax and payment terms

If those items are bundled into one neat total, the quote may still be legitimate, but it is not transparent enough to compare with another bid. Bundling makes it easy to miss where money is being saved — or where it will reappear later.

The most common hiding places are:

- **Hardware downgrades.** A cheaper bid may use lighter tracks, basic locks, or standard hinges where the better quote specifies heavier-duty parts.
- **Glazing changes.** A quote can quietly shift from double glazing to a simpler build-up, or from low-emissivity glass to standard glass.
- **Frame system differences.** One supplier may be pricing a standard residential profile, while another is allowing for a thermally broken or higher-capacity system.
- **Measurement assumptions.** A quote based on nominal sizes can look fine until custom fabrication is required.
- **Access assumptions.** Ground-floor access is one price; rear-lane access, narrow side setbacks, or upper-floor work is another.

None of those changes show up clearly if you only look at the bottom line.

## The three questions that expose a weak quote

The fastest way to test a quote is to ask these three things:

- What exactly is included in the installed price?
- What site conditions would trigger an extra charge?
- What is excluded, even if the exclusion seems minor?

A clear supplier can answer all three without hesitation. A vague supplier will drift into generalities, which usually means the price has been built on assumptions rather than measurements.

If the answer to any of those questions is something like 'we'll work that out on site,' the quote is not finished. It may be a rough estimate, but it should not be treated as a final comparison number.

## When the cheapest quote really is the right one

There are cases where the low bid is the right bid. That happens when the scope is simple, the measurements are standard, the access is easy, and every supplier is quoting the same specification. A straightforward ground-floor replacement with no finishes, no scaffold, and no structural changes can be compared pretty cleanly.

But the moment the job gets more complicated, the low bid loses its meaning unless the scope stays identical. On heritage homes, coastal properties, second-story replacements, or projects with custom sizes, the cheapest number is often just the least complete number.

## The comparison rule that protects your budget

The safest rule is simple: never compare a quote until every missing item has been priced back in.

That means normalizing the bids to the same baseline before deciding who is actually cheaper. Once you do that, the real value becomes visible very quickly. A quote with more detail usually costs less in the end because it leaves fewer gaps for surprises to live in. A thin quote often looks efficient only because the missing pieces have not been charged yet.

The most useful question is not 'who gave the lowest number?' It is 'which quote describes the actual job most completely?'

That is the difference between a budget you can trust and a figure that grows after the installer arrives.
