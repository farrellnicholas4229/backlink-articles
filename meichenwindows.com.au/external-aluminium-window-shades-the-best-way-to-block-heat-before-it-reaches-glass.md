# External Aluminium Window Shades: The Best Way to Block Heat Before It Reaches Glass

## Heat Has to Be Stopped Outside the Glass

The most important fact about window heat is simple: glass is not the place to deal with it.

Solar energy arrives as short-wave radiation. Clear glazing lets most of that energy pass through, where it strikes floors, walls, furniture, and countertops. Those surfaces warm up and re-radiate heat as long-wave infrared. Once that happens, the glass becomes a trap. A window that looked harmless in the morning can behave like a compact solar heater by midafternoon.

That is why the location of the shading device matters more than almost anything else. Shade outside the glass and the heat never crosses the building envelope. Shade inside the glass and the heat is already in the room.

On retrofit work, the difference shows up fastest in west-facing rooms. A living room with interior blinds may look darker, but the air still feels hot, the perimeter zone stays uncomfortable, and the air conditioner keeps cycling. Put the shade outside and the same space can feel dramatically calmer without changing the glass, the insulation, or the HVAC system.

## Why Interior Blinds Never Catch Up

Interior blinds and curtains do one job well: they control glare and privacy. They do not do a good job of stopping solar heat gain.

Once sunlight has already passed through the glass, the blind can only intercept the light and heat after it has entered the room. If the blind is dark, it absorbs radiation and warms up. If it is light, it reflects some light but still sits in a hot air pocket against the window. In both cases, the absorbed heat eventually leaks back into the room.

That is the flaw in the common assumption that any shade is better than no shade. It is not just a matter of how much sunlight is blocked. It is a matter of *where* the blockage happens.

A few practical differences explain the gap:

- Interior shades reduce glare, but the glass still gets hot.
- Curtains often create a pocket of trapped air that becomes a secondary heat source.
- Blackout fabrics may make a room darker, yet they can also absorb more heat.
- The closer a blind sits to the room side of the glass, the less useful it becomes for thermal control.

A large west-facing window can admit a surprising amount of heat in late afternoon. Even a modest opening can behave like a radiator when the sun angle is low and direct. That is why the room may feel stuffy even when the air temperature has not yet climbed to its daily peak.

## What External Aluminium Shades Actually Do

That is the logic behind [external aluminium window shades](https://meichenwindows.com.au/external-aluminium-window-shades/): they intercept radiation before the glazing ever turns it into indoor heat.

Aluminium is well suited to that job because it is light, stable, and highly reflective when finished properly. A powder-coated or anodized exterior surface can reflect a substantial share of incoming solar energy back toward the outside environment. Whatever heat is absorbed is released outdoors through airflow rather than being stored inside the room.

This is the key physical advantage:

- The sun hits the shade first, not the glass.
- The shade absorbs or reflects the load outside the building envelope.
- Wind and outdoor air carry away the remaining heat.
- The window glass stays cooler, which means less heat is transmitted inward.

That sequence changes the entire thermal behavior of the opening. A room with external shading is not simply darker. It has a lower heat input to begin with.

The material matters here, but placement matters more. A mediocre exterior system will usually outperform a perfect interior system because the geometry is on its side. The same principle explains why fixed awnings, louvres, and exterior roller systems can all feel so effective even when they look relatively simple from the street.

## The Geometry Matters More Than the Product Label

The best solar shade is not defined by how expensive it is. It is defined by how well it matches the sun path.

A fixed overhang works well when the sun is high, especially on north-facing glass in climates with strong summer sun. Louvred systems handle a wider range of angles because the blades can block direct rays while still admitting daylight and airflow. Adjustable external shades are useful when the solar load changes through the day and through the seasons.

The common design mistake is to think of shading as decoration added after the window decision is already made. In reality, the shade has to be sized and positioned to stop direct sun from reaching the glass at the most critical times.

That usually comes down to a few variables:

- **Projection depth**: a shallow shade may look adequate but still allow low-angle sun to sneak under or around it.
- **Side coverage**: east- and west-facing openings often need more than a simple top overhang because the sun arrives at an angle.
- **Mounting position**: the farther outside the shade sits, the more effective it becomes at blocking heat before it enters.
- **Blade angle or slope**: the correct angle can block direct rays without killing daylight.

A good design blocks the sun when the room needs relief and allows useful daylight when the heat load is lower. That balance is what makes external shading a performance element rather than just an accessory.

## Why the Comfort Difference Is Bigger Than People Expect

Air temperature is only part of what people feel near a window. Radiant heat matters too.

A person sitting beside unshaded glass can feel hot even when the thermostat shows a reasonable room temperature. That happens because the body is exchanging heat with the hot surface of the glass and the surrounding sunlit objects. The room may technically be within the setpoint, but the occupant still feels overheated.

External shading cuts that problem at the source. The glass stays cooler, the nearby surfaces stay cooler, and the perimeter zone becomes usable again. In homes, that means a couch beside a sun-facing window is no longer the worst seat in the house. In offices, it means fewer complaints from the desks near the facade. In bedrooms, it means the room stops storing the afternoon sun and releasing it long after sunset.

The energy effect follows the comfort effect. When solar gain is reduced before it enters the room, the cooling system does less work. That can lower peak demand on hot afternoons, reduce compressor cycling, and make temperature control more even across the room.

For buildings with large areas of glass, the difference can be substantial because every square foot of unshaded glazing acts like an amplifier for solar load. Once that energy is inside, air conditioning has to remove it. If the energy never crosses the glass, there is nothing to remove.

## The Best Use Cases Are Usually the Most Obvious Ones

Some windows benefit more than others, and the winners are predictable.

- **West-facing windows**: These are often the biggest problem because afternoon sun arrives at a low angle and hits hard when the building has already been warming all day.
- **East-facing windows**: Morning sun can create a fast heat spike in kitchens, bedrooms, and breakfast areas.
- **Large glazed living areas**: Open-plan rooms with broad glass panels tend to overheat quickly without exterior shading.
- **Bedrooms with direct sun**: A room can be darkened with interior blinds, yet still hold heat well into the night.

On these openings, exterior shading does more than improve comfort. It changes how the room is used. A west-facing lounge becomes livable in the afternoon. A bedroom cools down faster after sunset. A home office stays more stable during the hours when concentration usually drops.

That is why many builders treat exterior shading as a first-line climate control measure rather than a finishing touch. Once the glass is chosen and the wall is built, adding an exterior barrier is often the most effective way to improve performance without changing the architecture.

## What To Prioritize When the Goal Is Heat Control

If the purpose is thermal control rather than privacy or decoration, the decision hierarchy is straightforward.

1. **Put the shade outside the glass.**
2. **Match the shade to the sun path of the window.**
3. **Favor rigid, reflective, weather-resistant materials.**
4. **Use interior blinds only as a second layer for privacy or light control.**

That order matters because each layer solves a different problem. Exterior aluminium shades handle heat. Interior blinds handle glare, darkness, and privacy. Confusing the two usually leads to disappointment, especially in rooms that receive direct sun for several hours a day.

The most cost-effective strategy is often to spend on the exterior layer first. A well-placed outside shade can reduce the load on the whole room, which then makes any interior treatment more of a comfort feature than a desperate attempt to compensate for the sun.

## The Test That Reveals the Whole Truth

Stand beside a sunlit window at the time of day when the room usually overheats.

If the glass itself feels hot and the area around it radiates warmth, the room is already dealing with heat that has crossed the glazing. If an exterior shade removes that sensation while an interior blind does not, the physics have made the decision for you.

Heat that never reaches the glass never needs to be removed by the air conditioner. That is the entire advantage of external shading, and it is why exterior aluminium systems keep outperforming interior fixes whenever solar gain is the real problem.
