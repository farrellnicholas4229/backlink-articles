# Aluminum Windows Made to Measure: Why Tolerance Matters Most

## The real product is not the frame size

The promise of [aluminum windows made to measure](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-windows-made-to-measure/) is not exactness for its own sake. The real value is that the frame can be built around a building opening with enough clearance to install cleanly, enough compression to seal properly, and enough rigidity to hold its shape for decades.

That sounds simple until a job lands on site. A rough opening is rarely a perfect rectangle. Brickwork moves, timber settles, render swells, and a wall that looked square on paper can be several millimeters out by the time the old frame comes out. On a 2,100 mm opening, a 4 mm difference between the top and bottom may look trivial to the eye. In practice, it changes how the sash locks, how the seal compresses, and whether the installer can keep the frame plumb without forcing it.

A custom window order is not asking a fabricator to copy a number. It is asking them to solve a geometry problem.

## Why exact numbers are not enough

A lot of buyers think the job starts and ends with width and height. Those numbers matter, but they do not tell the full story.

The opening also needs:
- square corners, or at least a clear understanding of how far out of square it is
- plumb jambs so the frame does not twist under load
- a level sill or a deliberate plan for drainage and packers
- reveal depth that matches the profile system
- room for sealant, fasteners, and installation adjustment

If any of those are missing, the frame may still go in, but the installation becomes a compromise. And compromise is where many aluminum window problems begin: sash binding, uneven reveals, poor weather sealing, visible packing, and hardware that wears faster because the frame is under constant stress.

A good supplier of [custom aluminum windows](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-windows-made-to-measure/) should care as much about the opening as about the finished size. The measurement sheet is not just a quote document. It is the engineering brief.

## Why aluminum exposes bad measurement faster than timber does

Timber can hide sins that aluminum makes obvious. A skilled carpenter can plane a tight spot, trim a reveal, or make up a few millimeters with site work. Aluminum does not behave that way. It is rigid, stable, and engineered to hold a precise shape. That is exactly why it performs so well over time, but it also means it will not forgive a sloppy opening.

If the frame is too tight, installers start forcing it into position. That can twist the rectangle enough to change sash alignment by the time the fasteners are tightened. The result may not be visible on day one, but it shows up later as stiff operation, latch misalignment, or compression seals that never settle correctly.

If the frame is too loose, the opposite problem appears. There is too much packing, too much sealant, and too much visible variation around the perimeter. On larger openings, that can create a patchwork look even when the product itself is high quality. The owner sees a premium powder-coated frame, but the eye keeps landing on the uneven edge detail.

That is why the right tolerance is more important than the exact number on the tape. A made-to-measure window should be sized to the opening as it actually exists, not as the drawing hoped it would be.

## The gap around the frame is part of performance

People often react to installation clearance as if any gap means the measurement was wrong. In reality, the gap is part of the system. It gives installers room for shims, fixings, sealant, and minor building movement. It also allows for thermal expansion and contraction, which matters even in stable aluminum systems.

The important point is that the gap has to be intentional. Too little room and the frame gets stressed. Too much room and the sealant joint becomes overworked or visually messy. The ideal allowance depends on the profile, the substrate, and whether the opening is a retrofit in old masonry or a new-build frame with accurate set-out.

This is where made-to-measure becomes more than a manufacturing label. It becomes a coordination process between the building, the fabricator, and the installer. The best result happens when all three are looking at the same geometry, not just the same nominal dimensions.

## What gets missed when measurement is treated as a formality

The smallest measurement errors usually come from assumptions.

One opening may be taken from the interior plaster line, while another is taken from the structural opening. One person measures after demolition, another before patching. One side of the wall may be bowed, but only the centerline was checked. The numbers appear tidy until the frame arrives and the site discovers that the reveal depth is different from what the order assumed.

The problems most often show up in three places:
- heritage homes where walls are rarely square
- renovations where old frames were removed and the opening was altered on the fly
- large-format glazing where even minor misalignment becomes obvious across a wide span

On these jobs, a single measurement set is not enough. Diagonals, depth, substrate condition, and the planned finish all matter. A frame that is perfect on paper can still be the wrong product if it cannot be installed without forcing the opening or rebuilding the surround.

## The supplier questions that prevent expensive mistakes

A serious order process should feel more like a technical review than a simple sales call. Before fabrication, the supplier should know:
- whether the opening is new, existing, or altered
- how far out of square the diagonals are
- what the final finish will be around the reveal
- whether the frame sits into masonry, timber, steel, or a subframe
- what clearance is needed for drainage, fixings, and sealing
- whether the opening will carry operable sashes, fixed glass, or both

Those questions are not administrative overhead. They are the difference between a frame that fits once and a frame that performs for years.

The phrase [aluminum windows made to measure](https://meichenwindows.com.au/aluminium-windows-made-to-measure/) sounds like a product category, but in practice it describes a measurement discipline. The frame can only be as accurate as the opening data behind it. The opening data can only be as good as the person who understands what matters beyond width and height.

## The practical rule that avoids most failures

Measure the building as it is, not as it should be. Then build in the tolerance that lets the frame be installed without stress.

That one rule solves most of the problems people wrongly blame on fabrication quality. A premium aluminum system installed into a poor opening will still disappoint. A well-measured opening matched to a properly specified frame will look cleaner, seal better, and operate more smoothly from the first day of use.

That is the hidden truth behind made-to-measure work: the real craft is not producing a custom rectangle. It is creating the space for that rectangle to work.
